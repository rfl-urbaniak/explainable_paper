A Computationally Feasible Framework for  Causal Probabilistic Explanation
Rafal Urbaniak, Sam Witty, Daniel Waxman, Andy Zane, John Feser, Poorva Garg, Drew Lehe, Eli Bingham  
16 Dec 2025 (modified: 10 Mar 2026)Submitted to CLeaR 2026Conference, Area Chairs, Reviewers, AuthorsRevisionsBibTeXCC BY 4.0
Keywords: Probabilistic Causal Models, Explanation, Responsibility, Actual Causality, Probability of Necessity and Sufficiency
Abstract: 
Causal
 attribution and explanation are mainstays of philosophical, scientific,
 and policy analysis. Some current frameworks, however, such as actual 
causality, suffer from an inability to scale causal reasoning to large 
problems, while attribution methods that do scale---such as SHAP or 
LIME---are not causally aware. To address this two-fold problem, we 
present Probabilistic Causal Impact (PCI). Inspired by Halpern and 
Pearl's actual causality and Pearl's probability of necessary and 
sufficient causation, PCI provides tractable causal explanations in 
causal probabilistic machine learning models. By the same token, PCI 
provides explanations for events in the world, insofar as the model 
adequately represents real mechanisms. By re-conceptualizing explanation
 as an estimation problem on an expanded, causal probabilistic model, 
PCI allows for the use of well-established approximation algorithms, 
unlocking computationally tractable, truly causal explanations for large
 models. We test PCI on scaled-up, but canonical test cases for actual 
causation involving overdetermination and undercutting, finding that we 
recover results deemed intuitive by the literature, and illustrate that 
the correct behavior is preserved in the continuous version of the 
problem. We apply PCI to a real-life causal spatio-temporal model with 
complex machine-learned components trained on millions of data points. 
In this setting, we compare PCI to the non-causal (yet scalable) 
attribution method SHAP, and find that our approach yields explanations 
that agree with domain expert opinion, while SHAP does not.

Review: 
Summary. This work claims the following contributions:


    

  1. A method called Probabilistic Causal Impact (PCI) that provides 
"tractable causal explanations in causal probabilistic machine learning 
models," inspired by Halpern and Pearl's actual causation and Pearl's 
probabilities of necessity and sufficiency.
    2. 

  2. Empirical tests on "scaled-up, but canonical test cases for actual 
causation involving overdetermination and undercutting," recovering 
results "deemed intuitive by the literature," and illustrating extension
 to the continuous version of the problem.
    3. 

  3. Empirical results on a "real-life causal spatio-temporal model with 
complex machine-learned components trained on millions of data points," 
which show superior agreement with domain expert opinion when compared 
to SHAP.
    4. 



Originality. The paper seems to oversell its 
novelty. Several methods already claim to tractably provide causal 
probabilistic explanations [1-6], the core contribution of this work, 
but no empirical or theoretical comparison to these methods is provided.
 The authors themselves characterize PCI as a "natural expansion" of 
existing ideas, noting they are "just standing on [prior work's] 
shoulders".


Significance. The intersection of XAI, causality, 
and tractable inference is an important problem area. However, the 
significance of this work is tempered by the existence of many prior 
works addressing this same intersection, none of which are discussed, 
along with issues with technical specification of the method. In 
addition, a direct comparison with PN / PNS is not made.


Quality. The primary claims lack sufficient supporting evidence:


    

  • Contribution 1 (PCI method): The quantity is novel, but the theorems require assumptions not satisfied in experiments.
    • 

  • Contribution 2 (benchmarks): The discrete benchmark is acknowledged 
to be "easy," with an exploitable structure, and no sample complexity 
analysis is provided for the approximation procedure. The continuous 
benchmark lacks motivation for its stated qualitative expectations, as 
discussed below.
    • 

  • Contribution 3 (real-world comparison): No quantitative results are provided.
    • 



Clarity. The paper is challenging to follow. 
Definitions in Section 2 are presented without motivation, and some 
notation is overloaded (e.g., Y used for two different purposes in 
Definition 5). Theorems 10 and 11 in Section 3 lack formal specification
 in the main text, including specification of assumptions. The method in
 Section 4 is described informally through bullet points rather than as a
 formal algorithm, using several undefined terms. The causal impact 
function, a key component of the definition of PCI, is selected without 
discussion.


Detailed Comments and Questions:


    

  1. (Sec 1) How does PCI compare theoretically and empirically to the causal Shapley literature [1-6]?
    

    2. 

  2. (Sec 1) The work claims PCI was "inspired by the study of 
descriptive normality" [7,8]. How exactly does PCI relate to the 
examples proposed in these works? Does PCI resolve the counterexamples 
to standard actual causation put forth there?
    

    3. 

  3. (Sec 2) Regarding the cardinality-constrained uniform selection (Example 1):
    

    a. Why does it make sense to use a cardinality-constrained 
distribution rather than any other distribution - such as, for instance,
 a uniform distribution over all subsets?
b. How would one in general justify choosing J≠1 or K≠|X|?
    

    4. 

  4. (Sec 2) The work does not motivate the use of the ϵ-
excised alternative distribution (Example 2), rather than, for instance,
 the observational distribution, as is done in SHAP-related literature. 
It would be helpful to understand why the former is preferable to the 
latter.
    

    5. 

  5. (Sec 3) PCI is defined generally with a ci function that takes both sufficiency (ys) and necessity (yn) terms, claimed as a generalization of PNS. However, Theorems 10 and 11 require $ci(y^s, y^n, y^) = \mathbb{I}(y^n \neq y^)$,
 which ignores sufficiency entirely. Section 4 also explicitly drops 
sufficiency. Where is the full PCI (with sufficiency) validated?
    

    6. 

  6. (Sec 3-4) Theorems 10 and 11 require the alternative value 
distribution to be "fully supported on non-factive values." The 
stone-throwing benchmark in Section 4 appears to violate this: if the 
alternative samples all throwers as not throwing, the bottle cannot be 
hit. Do the experiments satisfy the theorems' conditions?
    

    7. 

  7. (Sec 4) Several terms in the "Approximate estimation" paragraph are used without definition:
    

    a. "existential necessity claim witness"
b. "active antecedent"
c. "reject-condition" (in "reject-condition the necessity world sample")
d. "blame" in "blame the variable that has a higher mean score"
    

    8. 

  8. (Sec 4) What is the time complexity per sample, and what sample complexity is required to achieve a given error bound?
    

    9. 

  9. (Sec 5) New notation "ci(V)" is introduced for "sample mean 
causal impact" without explaining its relationship to Equation (4) or 
the Examples afterwards. The ci() function used in this section is also 
not specified.
    

    10. 

  10. (Sec 5) The paper states "We do not expect to see zero estimates 
for irrelevant properties, as the score also results from sampling other
 potential causes and witnesses," but this explanation does not clarify 
why sampling other causes would inflate scores for irrelevant variables.
 Moreover, non-zero attribution to irrelevant variables appears to be 
undesirable given the minimality intuition underlying actual causation.
    

    11. 

  11. (Sec 5) The qualitative expectations for continuous causal impact
 are stated without motivation. It is unclear why ci(A1) and ci(A2) 
should be closer to each other than to ci(A0), why ci(A1) > ci(A2) when A1≫A2, and why the threshold "<2" is meaningful (the paper says "let's explicate this proximity as the absolute difference between sampled values being <2," which appears arbitrary).
    

    12. 

  12. (Sec 6) The work claims PCI shows superior agreement with domain 
expert opinion compared to SHAP, but provides no figure, table, or 
quantitative metrics on this agreement, despite claiming to "show 
performance results for a prototype model." A DAG of 25 nodes and 99 
edges is mentioned, but not shown. Model details are withheld because 
the work is "related to ongoing commercial work." How was expertise 
determined, how many experts were consulted, what was the evaluation 
protocol, and can you provide quantitative results, including comparison
 on the mentioned baselines [1-6]?
    

    13.

Rebuttal: 
We thank the reviewer for their careful reading of the paper and for the detailed and constructive feedback.


High-level concerns and the scope considerations


The goal of this paper is to introduce and formalize Probabilistic Causal Impact (PCI)
 as a general computational framework for causal explanation in 
probabilistic causal models, and to situate it primarily with respect to
 established approaches in probabilistic causality—most notably actual causation (AC) and  (PN/PS/PNS).
 To support the claim that PCI generalizes these notions, we show how 
each arises as a special case of our framework under explicit 
simplifying assumptions (e.g., outcome discretization in both cases; 
neglect of the sufficiency world and variable discretization in AC; and 
omission of witness nodes in PN/PNS). 


Accordingly, the paper focuses on defining PCI, clarifying its 
conceptual relationship to AC and PN/PNS (the latter being present in 
Example 3), and demonstrating its computational feasibility
 on canonical causal benchmarks involving overdetermination and 
undercutting, as well as on a scaled-up real-world model. The 
originality of the approach results from the fact that so far in the 
causal modeling literature: (i) there is no expansion of ideas 
underlying AC to continuous variables and approximate search through 
witness sets in a computationally feasible fashion, and (ii) there is no
 computationally feasible expansion of the PN/PS strategy to deal with 
examples akin to those dealt with AC, where symmetries need to be broken
 by a strategy akin to mediation analysis.


We agree that causal Shapley-style approaches are relevant, and we 
will add a short discussion situating PCI with respect to this 
literature and clarifying points of conceptual and methodological 
divergence. We will also extend the conclusion section with more 
explicit discussion of further Work directions and the current paper's 
scope limitations.


Connection to causal SHAP


Referring to papers cited by the referee,
Janzing et al. argue that conditioning is conceptually wrong for feature
removal, and that dropping a feature should be interpreted causally as an intervention. 
Sharma et al. propose CF-Shapley to attribute a change in a system metric from a reference y0 to an observed yt: ΔY:=yt−y0, using a structural causal model.  For input Xi with observed xi and reference xi0, the CF-Shapley value is the average counterfactual effect of setting Xi to its reference value across all subsets S of other variables: ϕi=∑S:i∉S1n(n−1|S|)(Ydo(XS=xS0)(u)−Ydo(XS=xS0,Xi=xi0)(u)). A similar estimand is introduced by  Heskes et al., except it's defined in terms of permutations.


We agree that introducing interventional semantics is an improvement,
 but there are key conceptual differences between this approach and PCI:


    

  1. Different questions. SHAP (and causal SHAP) 
measures a feature’s contribution relative to a group baseline. PCI 
measures how a feature contributes to a unit’s prediction being close to
 factual value and how changing it would lead that prediction away from it. For a nearly average observation where yo≈f(x0), SHAP attempts to explain near-zero values, while PCI asks which features keep the prediction near yo
 and which would move it away. The only "baseline" in PCI is the local 
factual value, which makes the attribution even more focused on 
token-level causality.
    

    2. 

  2. Computation. SHAP requires combinatorial 
averaging over permutations/subsets. PCI estimands are expectations over
 probabilistic models, enabling standard inference methods.
    

    3. 

  3. Mediation-like witness blocking. PCI uses 
mechanisms inspired by overdetermination and undercutting to break 
symmetries (as illustrated in benchmarks) and yield finer responsibility
 attributions. Causal SHAP lacks these.
    

    4. 

  4. Connection. Our conjecture is that with null 
witness proposals, empirical distributions, uniform sampling over 
candidate sets, and a baseline-distance metric, PCI can be in fact used 
to approximate estimands closely related to causal SHAP values.
    

    5. 



We will add an explicit discussion of these points and of Causal SHAP more generally to the paper.


Relationship between AC, experiments and theorems


We apologize for any lack of clarity in the statement of the theorems. They require that the alternative values of intervention candidates have full support, that is, that any non-factual value from the range considered as reasonable alternative interventions
 has a non-zero probability/density. In the stone-throwing example, this
 means that the probability of Sally not throwing and that the 
probability of Bill not throwing should be non-zero. These conditions 
are satisfied in the stochastic model under consideration.


What is not required by the theorems is that the outcome 
variable conditional on each particular interventional setting should 
have full support. This will obviously be false in any deterministic 
SCM, but it is not required by the theorems.


We will inspect the wording of the theorems and the discussion thereof, ensuring that it doesn't suggest this misinterpretation.


Motivations for the causal impact function


The metric we use here is a generalization of standard estimands that
 rely on L1 distance.
In a whole array of causal effect literature, L1 distance between 
outcomes is an outcome of interest (e.g. in Average Treatment Effect, 
Conditional Treatment Effect, L1 distance is also used in SHAP). 


While we depart from those approaches by moving away from having a 
single baseline value and integrating over the empirically informed 
conditional distribution of alternative values, we preserve the spirit 
thereof by still using L1, except now in two dimensions. This being 
said, if the users have a reason to conceptualize treatment effect using
 L2 or some other distance measure, this is fully composable and they 
are free to do so.


We will clarify this further in the text.


Cardinality-constrained search distributions


In a sufficiently interesting model with many variables, sampling 
interventions uniformly from the powerset would have the consequence of 
the expected size of intervention set being n/2 where n is the number of variables. That would mean, say, that to judge the role of a single feature F
 in a model with 50 variables, one would be looking mostly at 
interventions involving 25 variables and trying to disentangle the role 
of F. In a realistic model the noise involved will not allow one to do this reliably.


This would, moreover, not be conceptually the right thing to do. 
Imagine a model with 50 variables each corresponding to a particular 
voter (out fo a group fo 50) voting "yes"/"no".
Suppose the total outcome is "yes" and we want to inspect the role of a 
particular voter. Sampling uniformly from the powerset, the expected 
size of alternatives intervened on would be 25, so this procedure would 
force us to bias the number of voters changing their mind in the 
alternative worlds we consider to be close to 25.


Why use sets at all instead of single witnesses and single 
interventions? This is analogous to model-building choices: many models 
go beyond "single feature roles" by allowing pairwise interaction terms.
 Why only pairwise? Because it's hard to add all possible n-feature 
interaction terms. In allowing for up to n>2 terms, we generalize 
slightly allowing for up to four-term interactions, but limiting 
considerations are analogous.


Motivations for excision


The core insight from the probabilities of causal necessity and 
sufficiency is that responsibility attributions have two components: how
 much causal power does a property have to make things happen the way 
they did, and how much causal power does a property have to make things 
different if we intervene for it to have a different value. Conceptually
 this is a generalization of the common pattern in causal effect 
estimands: one always compares treatment to some baseline different from the treatment.
 The generalization here is that instead of comparing to a single 
sometimes somewhat arbitrarily chosen baseline, in a continuous case we 
integrate over alternative values together given an informed posterior 
over them.


To build up intuition, consider the limiting case of a binary 
variable. For instance, in asking whether a person's health outcome is 
impacted by a given drug dosage, the intuition is that we should compare
 this to regimes in which we excise, i.e. do not give that drug to the 
subject. To investigate the sufficiency of the drug, we separately investigate the consequence of giving the drug to the subject. 


Some need to choose a meaningful alternative value already comes up 
as soon as one wants to distinguish treatments in a more interesting 
non-binary space: a decision about what a meaningfully different 
treatment is has to be tacitly made anyway - our framework forces the 
user to be more explicit about it.


Overall clarity


We thank the reviewers for feedback on clarity in the current form of
 the manuscript. We will take the following actions to help improve the 
clarity:


    

  • provide better motivation for the definitions in Section 2
    • 

  • move formal specification of theorems from the appendix to the main body of the paper
    • 

  • convert the method description in Section 4 into a more formalized algorithm
    • 

  • we will drop references to the notion of normality as that requires discussion that is too involved for this paper
    • 

  • extend the discussion of complexity and clean up notation around ci
    • 

  • explain how model noise in interesting models naturally leads to non-zero baseline ci.
    • 

  • expand the continuous benchmark into a tutorial with more extensive discussion of assumptions and underlying intuitions
    • 

  • provide graph, PCI and SHAP values for the real-life application, and better describe the setup
    •

Summary


The article introduces Probabilistic Causal Impact (PCI), a method 
that is computationally suitable while also producing causal 
explanations. This new method is based on well-established concepts such
 as probability of necessity and sufficiency. The computational 
feasibility of PCI is tested at scale, demonstrating that it can be 
applied to larger problems.


Strengths


    

  • The notation used throughout the paper is clear and easy to follow, 
making the technical content accessible to readers familiar with causal 
inference frameworks.
    • 

  • There is an appendix section that provides theoretical 
clarification, including a pedagogical explanation connecting the 
approach to Pearl's probabilities of causation and Halpern's actual 
causality. The notation remains consistent throughout the main text and 
appendices.
    • 

  • The paper introduces a new method of causal attribution that retains causal intuitions.
    • 

  • The proposed method is very universal - it does not restrict the 
domain of variables, the form of the model, distributions, or structural
 equations. This allows continuous and categorical variables to be used 
at the same time, and such models can comprise standard machine learning
 components like neural networks or Gaussian processes.
    • 

  • There is an approximation method that is feasible on larger graphs, 
which has been empirically evaluated. The experiments demonstrate that 
while exact actual causality computation times out at 17 variables, the 
approximate PCI method can handle models with 110-150 variables while 
maintaining good accuracy.
    • 

  • The method is applied to continuous variables in the Overdetermination and Undercutting Benchmark, showing good results.
    • 



Weaknesses and Questions


    

  • There is no discussion of how to choose the Alternative Value 
Distribution, for example with respect to the ε-ball in the excised 
distribution. While this is not necessarily a bad thing for a 
theoretical work, if the paper is trying to demonstrate potential use 
cases, it would be important to provide some guidelines and show what 
effects different choices can have on the resulting attributions.
    • 

  • Similarly, the Variable Selection Distribution is not discussed in 
depth, and the paper does not inform the reader whether different 
choices of this distribution will have impacts on the resulting 
attributions.
    • 

  • There is another component of the explanations - the Causal Impact 
Function, with the Absolute Difference Impact Score given as an example.
 Having this many parts that can be defined in multiple ways, without 
clear guidance on what constitutes a good choice - both formally and 
informally - makes it difficult for practitioners to apply the method 
confidently.
    • 

  • It is generally hard to tell what properties this method will have 
apart from being causal. More discussion of the theoretical guarantees 
or expected behavior under different configurations would strengthen the
 contribution.
    • 

  • Regarding Section 6 (Scaling Responsibility Attribution to a 
Real-world Automated Valuation Model) - while this could be an 
interesting study, there are too few details provided to draw meaningful
 conclusions. Additionally, the comparison to SHAP is not quantitative, 
and qualitatively the comparison lacks sufficient details to fully 
assess the claimed advantages of PCI over SHAP.
    • 



Please correct me if I have misunderstood any aspects of the paper or
 if the concerns raised above are addressed elsewhere in the work. I am 
happy to reconsider my score based on your response.

We thank the reviewer for their work. 


One high-level concern for the reviewer is clarity. In response, we 
will expand the continuous-variable example currently presented in the 
appendix into a more tutorial-style example that illustrates the PCI 
construction step by step, including a parallel comparison with SHAP.


To cast a bit more light of the motivations behind some decisions 
made in the writing of the paper, we add the following points (which 
will make their way into the paper).


Alternative distribution selection


We discuss in some length the motivations for excisions in our 
rebuttal to Reviewer S1iS. Those comments and the exposition here will 
find a way into the paper.


Here, let's think through other relevant considerations. There are a couple of outstanding questions here.


(1). Why not just use the empirical distribution?


Here the answer is widely Bayesian: whatever reasons one might have 
to form the posteriors using priors and conditioning on data, are the 
reasons why one should be using a posterior distribution instead of 
empirical distribution. A good guide for reasons to go this way can be 
found, for example, in Kruschke's book on Bayesian data analysis.


(3). Why only use  information upstream in shaping alternative distributions?


The base distributions for a given site are only informed by upstream
 variable values. This means that the exploratory search for alternative
 values is relatively wide but not unrealistic. We build in the 
assumption that the range of realistic alternative values for a feature 
is constrained by other factual features that have impact on it, but not
 by features below it in the causal diagram. Some alternatives to this, 
which can also be implemented are:


(i) use uniform distribution over the original distribution's support
 (in which case, for a person that is 17 years old, we would still give 
equal weight to scenarios in which, say, a person is 110 years old, as 
to scenarios in which the person is 25 years old, leading to rather 
unrealistic consequences), 


(ii) use posterior distribution conditional on all the other 
subject's features, which might be quite tight. For instance, it might 
be the case that given everything else we know about the person, e.g., 
that they are in high school, we can put pretty tight posterior over age
 (say, uniform with range 14-20). This fact should not force the user to
 use this posterior in providing semantics to "if their age was 
different..." to be constrained to only inspect values that belong to 
that range. In our approach, however, whether the person is in high 
school would be downstream of age in the causal graph, and so would not 
inform the search for alternative values.


(iii) use the model's marginal distribution over a given feature 
values. In this case, we would be providing less local explanation as 
our alternative scenarios would be exactly the same for all units of 
analysis and would not be informed by any particularities of a given 
case.


Benchmark selection, complexity considerations


The reason why this benchmark structure was chosen was that this is a
 minimal case involving both overdetermination and undercutting that 
generates causal responsibility asymmetry.  In that sense, it is 
conceptually hard, because it asks for correct responsibility 
attribution in the presence of those causal complexities. It is a 
classic structure used in the literature as a counterexample to 
straightforward counterfactual approaches to responsibility attribution,
 and also a source of cases where SHAP gives unintuitive results.


The reason why we say it's still relatively easy, is the fact that 
the set of witness sets that validate the restricted but-for clauses is 
closed uder the superset relation, which is a clearly defined 
simplifying formal constraint, simplifying witness search as this 
reduces the search space when it comes to identifying a witness set.


The sampling complexity grows with the model structure; sampling is 
just a forward pass through the model, so it is a function of whatever 
the graph complexity is. That graph complexity increases with problem 
size is clearly described in the paper.


One interpretation of the figure focusing on a comparison to AC is 
that on one hand we compute how many samples it would take to exactly 
compute the answer using AC, and the simulations illustrate how many it 
takes to get to the achieved level of performance. This affords an 
empirical sense of sample complexity.


Developing further theoretical insights into the framework remains an exciting area for future work. 


Theoretical exploration


Indeed, further theoretical exploration of how PCI relates to and 
generalizes existing methods is an important direction. That being said,
 there are some clear features that we can already say the method has:


    

  • It distinguishes causal sufficiency from causal necessity, and 
rewards/penalizes for differences from the factual in those two 
dimensions. 
    • 

  • It allows the user to break symmetries in models involving 
overdetermination and undercutting by employing a generalization of 
mediation analysis inspired by the way actual causality is defined
    • 

  • It is composable with Bayesian causal models
    • 

  • It is a generalization of actual causality and a generalization of 
the probability of sufficiency and the probability of necessity, thus 
inheriting most of the arguments for the use of those methods already 
present in the literature

Review: 
This
 paper introduces Probabilistic Causal Impact (PCI), a framework for 
causal explanation in probabilistic causal models. PCI generalizes ideas
 from Halpern–Pearl actual causality and Pearl’s probabilities of 
necessity and sufficiency, reframing causal explanation as an estimation
 problem over an expanded probabilistic causal model by enabling 
scalable approximation via Monte Carlo methods. The authors provide 
theoretical connections to actual causality, evaluate PCI on a toy 
problem as well as demonstrate scalability and qualitative advantages 
over SHAP on a real-world causal housing valuation model.


Strengths:


    

  • The paper offers a well-motivated synthesis of actual causality 
and probabilistic causation, with a novel reframing of explanation as an
 expectation over causal impact functions.
    

    • 

  • The connections to Halpern’s actual causality and Pearl’s notion 
of neccesity and sufficiency are carefully discussed, with formal 
results showing partial equivalence under reasonable assumptions.
    

    • 



Weaknesses:


    

  • What I am missing is an running example throughout the paper. 
Since the work is theoretical and notationally heavy and assumes deep 
familiarity with measure-theoretic probability and the actual causality 
literature an example-driven presentation would really improve the 
readability and accessibility of the work. 
    

    • 

  • The housing example relies primarily on agreement with domain 
expert intuition. While understandable given the setting, more 
systematic evaluation would help substantiate claims of superiority over
 SHAP.
    

    • 



Overall, I think the paper makes a nice contribution and should be accepted.

limited intuitive explanatory role, while PCI assigns non-trivial role 
to upstream variables (such as living area, the number of rooms etc.). 
We will also make sure we limit our claims to only involve facts we can 
disclose and explicitly report.


We will also make sure we systematically apply SHAP and include comparison to it in the other examples used in the paper.
